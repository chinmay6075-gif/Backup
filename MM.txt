What is AI Maturity Assessment?
AI maturity assessment evaluates your app teams readiness and capabilities across six key dimensions and It provides a comprehensive view of where you are today and a roadmap for where you need to go. The intent is not to mindlessly

Is this maturity model applicable to a portfolio, team, or application?
The primary assessment unit is the application level, because that is where evidence can be directly validated, including repositories, workflows, instructions, controls, agents, metrics, and operating practices.

Where multiple Scrum teams share the same repositories, workflows, controls, metrics, and operating model, they may be assessed as a single delivery unit. 

For applications delivered by multiple scrum teams, operating independently, an application may only achieve a maturity level if at least 80% of assessed teams satisfy that level and no assessed team is more than one maturity level below the claimed application rating.

L1	≥80% of teams achieve L1
L2	≥80% of teams achieve L2
L3	≥80% of teams achieve L3
L4	≥80% of teams achieve L4


Portfolios do not receive a direct maturity score. Portfolio views may be created by aggregating application maturity assessments for reporting purposes.

Does a legacy or brownfield application get graded on the same curve as a greenfield application?
Yes. All applications use the same maturity model and maturity levels.

However, assessment focuses on the team's ability to demonstrate the required capabilities, evidence, governance, adoption, and outcomes relative to their environment. Legacy applications should not be penalized simply because they begin from a less modern technical baseline

Does having GitHub Copilot, ACE, agents, or automation automatically increase maturity?
No.

Tool deployment is not maturity.

Maturity is earned through demonstrated adoption, evidence, governance, team operating model, readiness, and measurable outcomes. Teams must pass all mandatory assessment criteria for the claimed level. Assessment - How Is My Team Assessed? & AI Maturity Metrics

What determines a team's maturity level?
The first step is identifying the largest unit of SDLC work AI can repeatedly execute under governance.

This determines the candidate level.

The team must then pass all six mandatory assessment tests:

Engineering Capability
Adoption
Team Model & Roles
Readiness
Governance
Outcomes
The awarded maturity level is the level for which all mandatory requirements are satisfied. Assessment - How Is My Team Assessed?

If I use AI in just one SDLC phase, do I qualify for a maturity level?
No. AI Maturity is assessed across all six dimensions of the AI Maturity Model, not solely on AI usage within a single SDLC phase. To qualify for a maturity level, teams must meet the minimum requirements for every dimension associated with that level.

Why are the expectations phased this way?

The maturity model follows a deliberate progression based on risk, complexity, and organizational readiness:

Testing, documentation, and coding assistance typically offer the fastest return on investment with relatively low risk. AI can generate test cases, documentation, and implementation suggestions, while humans retain responsibility for review and approval.
Planning, requirements, and design activities require greater contextual understanding and directly influence downstream delivery outcomes. Effective AI delegation in these areas demands stronger specifications, governance, and architectural discipline.
Release, deployment, and operational activities introduce production and business risk. Meaningful AI delegation in these phases requires mature controls, observability, validation mechanisms, and clearly defined human accountability.
Architectural decision support and end-to-end autonomous workflows represent advanced maturity and are only expected at higher levels where governance, measurement, and operational safeguards are firmly established.
In summary: using AI extensively in a single SDLC phase does not automatically qualify a team for a maturity level. Maturity reflects consistent, evidence-based adoption across multiple SDLC phases and all six assessment dimensions, with broader delegation expectations as teams progress from L1 to L4.

What is the difference between Candidate Level and Awarded Level?
The candidate level identifies the highest maturity level the team may qualify for based on the largest unit of work delegated to AI.

The awarded maturity level is assigned only after all required evidence, adoption, readiness, governance, team model, and outcome requirements have been successfully demonstrated.

A team may demonstrate L3 engineering capability but still be assessed at L2 if other mandatory requirements are not met.

Why do all six assessment tests matter?
The model evaluates six dimensions together.

A team cannot compensate for weak governance, weak evidence, or poor outcomes with stronger adoption or automation.

Failure to satisfy any mandatory assessment criterion prevents the level from being awarded.

What is the difference between L2 and L3?
L2 (AI Augmented)

AI executes a controlled sequence of related tasks within an SDLC phase.

Humans still initiate workflows, coordinate work, supervise execution, and determine completion.

L3 (Agentic)

A governed agent executes an entire SDLC phase within defined permissions, controls, objectives, validation rules, and approval boundaries.

Humans supervise the phase, handle exceptions, and coordinate cross-phase handoffs.

The distinction is not whether an agent exists.

The distinction is the largest accountable unit of work AI can safely, repeatedly, and demonstrably complete under governance.

If I have agents, am I automatically L3?
No.

Many teams have agents but remain at L1 or L2.

An agent alone is not evidence of maturity.

To qualify for L3, the team must demonstrate phase-level execution and satisfy all six mandatory assessment tests

Is there a minimum required maturity level?
Currently, the model is intended to support learning, adoption, and progression.

At present, there is no enterprise-wide minimum required maturity level. The only requirement is to start and have a baseline for 2026.

Will assessment results be published?
Yes, in a central report

Future integration with an enterprise system such as DPM may be considered, but the publication model has not yet been finalized. 

What happens if we regress?
Maturity reflects the team's current operating state.

If requirements, governance requirements, architecture, risk profile, or ways of working change, a reassessment may result in a different maturity level.

This is treated as a normal reassessment outcome rather than a penalty. 

Can we request an exemption if our technology stack differs from the examples?
The maturity model describes required capabilities and outcomes, not a fixed technology stack.

Equivalent enterprise-approved patterns, tools, platforms, or approaches may be used if they satisfy the required maturity criteria. 

Are the metric targets mandatory pass/fail gates?
Metric ranges are target indicators of maturity and are expected to be demonstrated. Exceptions will have to be signed off by portfolio mgr and may be reviewed based on application context, risk profile, release cadence, architectural constraints, or regulatory requirements.

What tools are approved?
The approved tool landscape continues to evolve.

Teams should refer to enterprise AI guidance, approved enterprise platforms, and the enterprise AI portal for the latest supported tools and patterns.

Examples referenced throughout the maturity model are illustrative and do not represent an exhaustive list of approved technologies. 

How is the assessment performed?
The detailed assessment process is documented in the Assessment section of the maturity model.

What all should be included in repository level instruction file?
Repository-level instruction files should codify:

Coding Conventions
Style guides (indentation, naming, formatting)
Approved patterns and anti-patterns
Technology stack specifics (frameworks, libraries, versions)
A well maintained instructions.md file that describes the project's architecture, conventions and constraints shapes every interaction an AI agent has with the codebase
Testing Practices
Test structure requirements (unit vs. integration)
Logging and observability standards
Enforce a 'minimalist-first' Approach; Implement proper logging and reporting mechanisms
Validation and Documentation Standards
Acceptance criteria format
Documentation expectations
Quality gates and review expectations
Documentation stored in the same repository as application code improves an AI agent's understanding and highlights unique contextual details for target audiences
Organizational and Compliance Constraints 
Regulatory or safety-critical requirements 
Data residency and audit trail mandates
Integration patterns with legacy systems
Fail-safe protocols and operational constraints
Treating Instruction Files as Living Context Engineering Artifacts
These files should be versioned, reviewed, and maintained with the same rigor as code:

Version control: Every change is tracked and reversible
Peer review: Changes must be deliberate, not exploratory
Scope appropriately: Don't create one monolithic file; scope files to projects, modules, or directories so agents receive relevant context without overwhelming them
Evolve with feedback: When an agent fails or produces suboptimal output, add the missing context to the instruction file to prevent recurrence
Generate code that violates compliance requirements unknowingly
Diverge from architectural patterns you've established
Miss domain-specific constraints (safety modes, fail-safes, audit requirements)
Require constant rework and review
With them, agents become predictable collaborators aligned with your standards from the first output.

These files act as a procedural memory for agents, capturing institutional knowledge and evolving with feedback when agents fail tasks by adding necessary context to prevent repeat errors.

How often will teams be reassessed?
Teams may be reassessed when capabilities materially change, new evidence becomes available, or program governance requires reassessment(between a quarter to max a year)

What are the definitions of the metrics?
Lead Time for Change

Time from code commit to that code successfully running in production	Less than 1 day
Cycle Time	Time taken for a work item to move from “Committed” to “Done”	1–3 days
Defect Leakage Rate	% of defects found in production/UAT	Under 5%
Change Failure Rate	% of deployments to production that result in a failure requiring hotfix, rollback, or patch	0-15%

Can different maturity dimensions be at different levels?
Yes. A team may demonstrate stronger capability in one dimension than another.

However, maturity is awarded at level  all mandatory assessment requirements are satisfied.

Example:

Engineering Capability = L3
Adoption = L3
Governance = L2
Awarded maturity = L2
